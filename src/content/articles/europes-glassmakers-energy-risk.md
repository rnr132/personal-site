*By Rohit Nair, Director, Corporate Ratings*

The surge in European natural gas prices with the outbreak of war in the Middle East could transform a hitherto relatively stable year for European glass manufacturers into a challenging test of their hedging strategies and liquidity management.

Much depends on how the conflict lasts and the longer-term impact on energy prices, but it is glass manufacturers with higher levels of hedging that should be able to manage the spike in prices best. Those exposed to spot pricing will experience a faster deterioration in EBITDA and higher cash burn – hence, the uneven impact on credit metrics.

Companies with strong (above 85%) hedged portfolios for 2026 and partially hedged into 2027 should be able to weather the storm better. This is around the level of protection we estimate to be in place at most large manufacturers such as France's Saint-Gobain and Japan's AGC Glass which do not explicitly report the extent of hedging strategies.

Vidrala, the Spanish glass-packaging firm, had hedged 69% of its 2026 energy needs at end-December 2025, with 32% of 2027 costs hedged as well. Similarly, France's Verallia has a defined policy of hedging three years of costs in stages, with 100% of year-ahead costs hedged by the October of the previous year.

## Why the price spike matters so much for glass manufacturers

The glass sector provides a good snapshot of the challenges facing energy-intensive European industry as geopolitics continue to roil energy markets. Glass melting is a continuous, energy-intensive process with furnaces designed to run round-the-clock for years on end. Stopping a furnace is expensive, operationally risky and usually planned years in advance for assets approaching end-of-life. For unhedged manufacturers, energy cost volatility indicates short-term earnings volatility.

Additionally, while many packaging contracts may include provisions for energy surcharges, these adjustments often occur with delays. In highly competitive markets such as flat glass, there are limits to the ability to pass on prices. End consumers have already begun to push back against such contracts, and the ability of smaller manufacturers to effectively pass on costs remains limited.

European TTF gas prices have surged to around EUR 60/MWh having averaged around EUR 35-36/MWh for 2024 and 2025. Market expectations at the beginning of the year were for prices to average around EUR 32/MWh this year.

Given that 15-40% of the costs for glass production is directly comprised of energy costs, any unhedged production is now effectively loss-making. In addition, the shock comes at a time when architectural glass demand in Europe remains weak – most manufacturers had planned capacity reductions in response.

## Sector has limited near-term room for manoeuvre if energy prices rise even higher

If elevated prices persist and "USD 150 oil" tail scenarios become plausible, we expect limited flexibility in the initial one or two quarters for most glass manufacturers. Furnaces are difficult to stop, and pricing (via energy surcharges or renegotiations) generally resets with a contractual lag. As a result, unhedged exposure should reflect into EBITDA and cash flow until repricing catches up.

For most manufacturers, the practical levers for managing prices would continue to be commercial and financial in nature, not operational. These include accelerating surcharge mechanisms (via invocation of force majeure, if applicable), renegotiating pass-throughs on the revenue side and tightening working capital on the cost side.

We expect deferral of non-essential capex, and for larger companies to access liquidity. Smaller companies might seek waiver of covenants, especially for 2027, if energy prices stay high.

For major manufacturers in Europe, the debt profile is evenly spread, with an average of only 14% of overall debt coming due annually over the next four years, although there are some company-specific peaks and troughs.

## Low leverage, long-term contracts provide buffer for some European manufacturers

European glass manufacturers like Vidrala (reported leverage of 0.2x) and Saint-Gobain (reported leverage of 1.4x) should weather the impact of higher cash requirements for energy bills and potentially higher collateral for hedges better than competitors that are more highly levered.

Long-term contracts within in-built surcharges also provide protection in some segments of the industry such as the container glass market for fast-moving consumer goods and pharmaceutical clients. Such contracts enable companies to defend margins, albeit with a lag.

## Longer rise in energy prices will expose more leveraged manufacturers

Highly leveraged issuers in the glass sector with limited covenant headroom will inevitably face cashflow constraints if energy prices remain higher for longer amid a crisis-related slowdown in economic activity in Europe.

Most manufacturers should be able to ride out H1 FY 2026 with relatively lower impact on margins, but a prolongation of the energy shock takes them into territory where energy prices are unhedged or hedged at much higher costs, leading to prolonged margin erosion into 2027. This compounds cash burn and limits flexibility for debt refinancing for loans coming due in 2027 and 2028.

Also vulnerable are any manufacturers with plants with older furnace technology and limited fuel-switch flexibility.

Some segments face risk of higher input costs and weaker demand. Construction-linked flat glass is a cyclical product; if higher-for-longer energy prices lift inflation expectations and erodes business confidence, volume risk will compound the risk of squeezed profit margins.
